Cooking for a toddler who says no to everything

5 min read · Updated 30 September 2026

A toddler who refused yesterday's favourite is completely normal. What matters is how the adults respond at the table, and that everyone who feeds them responds the same way.

First, the good news

The NHS is clear that it's perfectly normal for toddlers to refuse food, or even to refuse to taste something new. It helps to judge a whole week rather than a single day. If your child is active, growing and looks well, they're probably getting what they need, even when it doesn't feel like it at dinner time.

What helps at the table

These come from the NHS advice on fussy eaters:

  • Serve the same family meal, without added salt, rather than a separate menu.
  • Eat together as often as you can. Toddlers copy what they see.
  • Keep portions small, and praise any bite, even a tiny one.
  • Offer refused foods again. Tastes change, and it can take many tries.
  • Change the form. A child who hates cooked carrot may eat it raw and grated.
  • Make it pleasant. A relaxed, sociable meal matters more than a clean plate.

What to avoid

  • Don't force it. If they refuse, calmly take the food away.
  • Don't use food as a reward. "Finish your vegetables and you get a biscuit" teaches that vegetables are the price and biscuits are the prize.
  • Don't compare with a sibling or a friend's child.
  • Keep snacks to about two a day, so they arrive at meals hungry.
  • Don't let them get overtired or over-hungry before a meal. Both make refusing more likely.

What a good day roughly looks like (ages 1 to 4)

FoodThe NHS suggestion
Milk and dairyAt least 350 ml of milk a day, or two servings of foods made from milk. Full-fat until age 2.
Fruit and vegetablesSome at every meal.
Starchy foodsBread, rice, pasta or potatoes.
ProteinMeat, fish, eggs, beans or lentils. Oily fish at least once a week.
DrinksWater or milk. No fizzy drinks. Juice isn't needed; if you give it, dilute it.
Sweet foodsWith a meal, not as a snack. No need to add salt.

Whole nuts and whole peanuts are still a choking risk until age 5, and small round foods like grapes should still be cut lengthways.

Small things that often work

These are practical ideas rather than official advice, so try them and keep what works:

  • Serve foods separately instead of mixed together. Many toddlers hate a mixed plate.
  • Put one familiar food on every plate, next to the new one.
  • Offer dips. Hummus, yoghurt or a mild tomato sauce make almost anything more interesting.
  • Let them help. Washing vegetables or stirring a bowl makes them more curious about the result.
  • Put a new food on a small side dish with no comment. No pressure means no argument.

Brief your helper, not just yourself

Fussy eating gets worse when the adults respond differently: one person coaxes, one bribes, one gives up. If your helper does much of the feeding, agree on three things and write them down:

  1. Small portions first, and more if they ask.
  2. No forcing, no bribes and no scolding. If they refuse, say "OK, maybe next time", take the plate away calmly and offer the next meal or snack at its normal time.
  3. Keep snacks to about two a day.

Explain the reason as well as the rule. In many households a well-fed child is a child who finished the plate, and chasing a toddler with a spoon is a sign of care. Your helper is trying to do right by your child, so a friendly "here's why we do it this way" goes much further than a list of don'ts. Put it in short, simple words, in the language they read best.

When to ask for help

Talk to your GP or health visitor if your child eats only a handful of foods, seems to be losing weight or not growing, gags or vomits on certain foods, or if mealtimes are causing real distress. It's always fine to ask.
